Prologue: Monday Morning at the Pawnshop

SCENE STATUS: RECONSTRUCTED (atmosphere and weekly pledge mechanics grounded in documented 1950s Dublin Liberties and Derry Bogside social histories; no invented dialogue)

Rain on Monday morning in inner-city Dublin did not fall so much as settle into wool. Along the narrow streets of the Liberties—between the brewery walls, the tenements, and the corner shops where groceries went onto a pencil-marked slate—women stood in quiet queues outside the three brass balls before nine o’clock. Under one arm sat a folded parcel wrapped in brown paper or newspaper: a husband’s Sunday overcoat, a heavy winter blanket, occasionally a wedding ring tucked inside a glove.

By Friday evening, if the weekly wage packet held, the same woman would walk back across the wet cobblestones to redeem the parcel before the shop shutters came down. By the following Monday morning, after coal, bread, milk, and rent had been counted out on the kitchen table, the coat went back across the counter.

A hundred and forty miles north, below the stone walls of Derry in the terraced lanes of the Bogside, the arithmetic was bleaker still. By the end of the decade, general unemployment in Derry hovered around twenty percent, and male unemployment approached thirty percent. Commercial banks did not open accounts for dockers, shirt-factory workers, or unemployed labourers without title deeds or salaried guarantors.

The Architecture of a Rigged Market

Under the Moneylenders Act, 1933 (No. 36 of 1933), the Oireachtas mandated licensing, required written contracts, banned compound interest, prohibited Sunday doorstep collections, and established a statutory interest threshold centred on 39 percent per annum. Yet statute books do not lend five pounds on a wet Tuesday night in Dublin 8. In the shadow economy beyond licensed counters, informal lenders operated on a brutal piece of street arithmetic known simply as “the shilling in the pound.”

Chapter One: The Empty Lunchboxes

SCENE STATUS: RECONSTRUCTED (grounded in Nora Herlihy’s documented teaching postings at Basin Lane and West Liffey Street national schools; no invented dialogue)

In the 1950s, a national school classroom in inner-city Dublin was a barometer of the street outside before a single sum was written on the blackboard. At Basin Lane in the Liberties and later at West Liffey Street, Nora Herlihy watched the same quiet evidence file through the door every morning: children arriving in damp coats, thin shoes patched with cardboard, and lunchboxes that carried bread without butter or nothing at all.

Herlihy was not a Dubliner by birth. She had been born in Ballydesmond, County Cork, on the rugged rural border between Cork and Kerry. She brought to the tenements of Dublin 8 the sharp, practical eye of rural Ireland—an eye that knew the difference between a family that refused to work and a family trapped in a broken economic machine.

Suppose a household borrowed £5 (100 shillings) from an unlicensed street lender at a shilling in the pound per week. Every Friday, five shillings left the table in interest alone. After twenty weeks, the family had handed the lender the entire £5 principal in interest—and still owed every penny of the original £5. Over a full year, a single shilling per pound per week extracted 52 shillings of interest on a 20-shilling loan: a 260 percent simple annual rate.

What Herlihy wanted was not alms. She wanted economic self-reliance—a legal, democratic instrument owned by the borrowers themselves. She found her co-conspirators—bakery worker Seán Forde and Kilkenny civil servant Séamus P. MacEoin—through the evening social-economics courses at University College Dublin.
